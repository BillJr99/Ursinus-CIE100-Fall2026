# Session Guide 4: Islamic Mystic Poetry (Th 9/17 and Tu 9/22)

*Instructor-facing session guide. Class meets Tu/Th 1:30 to 2:45 PM (75 minutes). Not for distribution to students.*

## Overview

This unit asks students to read devotional poetry from inside its tradition while examining what happens when that tradition is stripped away, a live issue given Rumi's strange afterlife as a decontextualized inspiration brand. The first session builds the necessary scaffolding (Sufism in brief, the conventions of the ghazal, the beloved as divine) and practices close reading; the second confronts the Instagram Rumi problem directly and gives students a small composition exercise in the mode of longing. The unit models how to engage a religious tradition with respect and analytical seriousness simultaneously.

## Learning Goals

Students should be able to explain how Sufi poetry uses the imagery of human love, wine, and separation to express longing for the divine, read a poem within the conventions of its tradition, evaluate what is gained and lost in popular decontextualized circulation, and compose a brief fragment in the mode of longing as a way of understanding the form from inside.

## Materials and Preparation

Prepare a brief (genuinely brief) orientation to Sufism and to the poets in the course booklet, written with care since you are not teaching religious studies but cannot responsibly skip context. Queue a short video of the Mevlevi sema (whirling ceremony) for Day 1, and prepare the Veiled Referent packets: for each group, three or four short passages of ecstatic love imagery from the booklet plus a slip listing the candidate referents (the human beloved, the divine, wine and intoxication, the teacher-guide, poetry itself). For Day 2, students bring the popular Rumi quotation from their writing prompt; prepare two or three side-by-side comparisons of a viral Rumi quote with a scholarly translation of the same passage, which takes some advance hunting but anchors the whole session.

## Session Arc, Day 1: The Beloved (75 minutes)

Open with fifteen minutes of orientation: who these poets were, what Sufism seeks, and why the conventions matter, including the standing ambiguity of whether the beloved is human, divine, or deliberately both. Spend ten minutes with the sema video, asking what it means that this tradition prays with the whole body, and connecting embodied devotion to the embodied poetics. Devote thirty-five minutes to the featured activity, The Veiled Referent, as detailed below: groups argue from specific lines which candidate referents their passages can sustain simultaneously, then report readings that honor the ambiguity rather than resolving it. Close with ten minutes of comparison back to Sappho, where the beloved was human and absence was geographic, and five minutes of commonplace writing.

## Session Arc, Day 2: Instagram Rumi (75 minutes)

Open with ten minutes collecting the viral quotations students brought onto the board. Run the decontextualization analysis for thirty minutes as detailed below. Spend twenty minutes on the broader question this opens: who may carry a tradition's texts, what translation owes its source, and whether popularity is a form of survival or of erasure, drawing the explicit line back to Sappho's fragments. Close with the composition exercise for fifteen minutes: each student writes a two to four line fragment addressed to something they long for without naming it, in the tradition's indirect mode, with volunteers reading aloud.

## Discussion Question Bank

Why does this tradition speak of God in the language of wine and lovers rather than the language of law and doctrine? What does separation mean when the beloved is divine, and is the longing itself the point? How should a twenty-first century American student read poems that are also prayers? What happens to "out beyond ideas of wrongdoing and rightdoing there is a field" when it appears on a yoga studio wall with the Islam removed? Is decontextualization a form of appreciation, appropriation, or both, and who has standing to say? Would the poets mind?

## Featured Activity Detail

**The Veiled Referent (Day 1, 35 minutes).** Each group receives three or four short passages of ecstatic love imagery from the course booklet and a set of candidate referents: the human beloved, the divine, wine and intoxication, the teacher-guide, and poetry itself. The task is deliberately not to pick the right answer: each group must argue from specific lines which referents the text can sustain simultaneously, marking the words that hold a double or triple reading open, and then report the strongest simultaneous reading to the class with the lines as evidence. Budget roughly twenty minutes for group work and fifteen for reports and debrief. The debrief names the insight directly: the veil is the point; mystic poetry resists single reference, and a reading that collapses the ambiguity to one referent has read less of the poem, not more. That principle is exactly what Day 2's decontextualization analysis will test, since the Instagram versions perform the collapse the groups just learned to refuse.

**The Instagram Rumi Problem (Day 2, 30 minutes).** Project a viral Rumi quotation beside a scholarly translation of the same passage with its religious vocabulary intact. In groups, students perform a difference audit: list every change (words, imagery, what was cut), then classify each change as translation, simplification, or erasure, and finally argue a verdict on what the popular version does to the poem. Groups then apply the same audit to the quotations they brought. The discussion this generates, about the best-selling poet in America being read almost entirely outside his tradition, is the unit's center of gravity, and it gives students a portable analytical move: ask of any quotation what was around it. Connect explicitly to Sappho, whose fragments were also curated by later hands, and forward to McLuhan, since the medium of the inspirational image post is itself shaping the message.

## Facilitation Notes

Some students in the room may be Muslim and may have standing relationships to these texts that deserve room without being put on the spot; never ask a student to speak for a tradition. Other students may bring reflexive suspicion toward religious texts, and the unit's framing, that we are reading as scholars who take the tradition seriously on its own terms, covers both. Keep the composition exercise invitational in tone; its purpose is formal understanding, not devotion. The revision workshop falls on Th 9/24, so remind students about Writing Fellow conferences.

## Connections Forward

Euthyphro arrives next and shifts from devotion to definition: from longing for the divine to interrogating what piety even is. Naming that pivot, from the heart's mode to the dialectic's mode, gives students a frame for why Plato can feel cold after Rumi, and why both modes might be necessary.
