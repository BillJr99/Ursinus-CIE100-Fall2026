# Session Guide 7: Genesis 1-22 (Tu 10/27 and Th 10/29)

*Instructor-facing session guide. Class meets Tu/Th 1:30 to 2:45 PM (75 minutes). Not for distribution to students.*

## Overview

Genesis is read here as literature and as the headwaters of questions the rest of the unit inherits: where the world came from, what humans are for, who holds the power to name, and what we are permitted to do with creation. Alter's translation, with its attention to the Hebrew's cadences and wordplay, lets the class treat the text closely without the session becoming either devotional or dismissive. Day 1 runs the creation accounts and the primeval history through a comparative gallery walk; Day 2 takes up the patriarchal narratives through the dominion-versus-stewardship debate and the Babel-and-naming thread that connects backward to Adichie and forward to McLuhan. The Essay 2 revision workshop shares Day 2.

## Learning Goals

Students should be able to distinguish the two creation accounts and articulate why the text preserves both, read Alter's translation choices as interpretive arguments, situate Genesis among other origin narratives without flattening any of them, analyze naming and language (Adam's naming, Babel) as claims about power, and conduct a structured debate on dominion and stewardship with textual evidence.

## Materials and Preparation

Day 1 needs the gallery walk stations prepared: short printed excerpts from three or four other origin accounts (Enuma Elish, a Popol Vuh passage, a one paragraph plain statement of Big Bang cosmology, and optionally a paragraph describing the simulation hypothesis), each posted with the same three analysis questions. Day 2 needs debate role cards and the Essay 2 drafts students bring for the workshop portion. Flag Alter's footnotes on key verses in advance.

## Session Arc, Day 1: Two Creations and a Flood (75 minutes)

Open with ten minutes on how to read scripture in a seminar: as a text with authors, seams, and artistry, an approach compatible with, though distinct from, reading it as scripture. Spend twenty minutes comparing Genesis 1 with Genesis 2 and 3 in close reading pairs: order of creation, names of God, the human role in each, with Alter's notes as evidence. Run the origins gallery walk for twenty-five minutes as detailed below. Use fifteen minutes for synthesis on what each account is for (liturgy, etiology, cosmology) and whether the question "which is true" is even well-formed across them, and close with five minutes of commonplace writing.

## Session Arc, Day 2: Naming, Babel, and the Binding (75 minutes)

Open with fifteen minutes on naming: Adam names the animals, the woman is named twice, and at Babel a single language is scattered; ask what the text believes naming does, and who names things now, from biological taxonomy to the labels on training data. Run the dominion-versus-stewardship debate for twenty-five minutes as detailed below. Spend fifteen minutes with Genesis 22, the binding of Isaac, read aloud slowly, with the Euthyphro dilemma explicitly on the table: the text where the dilemma's stakes become unbearable. Reserve fifteen minutes for the Essay 2 revision workshop in pairs, and five for exit writing.

## Discussion Question Bank

Why two creation accounts, and what does each make of the human? What does Alter's "welter and waste" preserve that "formless and void" loses? Is the serpent lying? What does the text think language is, that Babel should be God's response to unity? Is "fill the earth and subdue it" a license or a job description? What does Abraham's silence on Moriah mean, and would Euthyphro call him pious? Does Genesis read differently after Darwin, and should it?

## Featured Activity Detail

**The Origins Gallery Walk (Day 1, 25 minutes).** Stations around the room hold short excerpts from other origin accounts alongside Genesis 1. Groups rotate with the same three questions at every station: what existed before, what are humans for in this account, and what does this account ask of its hearers? The third question is the trapdoor, because it reveals that origin stories are rarely just explanations; they assign roles. The Big Bang station productively complicates the exercise, since students must decide whether a scientific account "asks" anything of them, and the simulation hypothesis station (if used) shows that technological cultures generate origin myths too. Synthesis names the move: every account of where we came from smuggles in an account of what we are for, which is precisely the contested ground of the Darwin and Deloria units ahead.

**Dominion or Stewardship (Day 2, 25 minutes).** A structured debate on Genesis 1:28 and 2:15. One side argues the text grants dominion in the strong sense; the other argues it commissions stewardship, "to till it and to watch it"; a third group of textual referees may interrupt only to demand a verse for any claim. After fifteen minutes of argument, dissolve the roles and ask the real question: this verse has been cited for centuries in arguments about land, animals, and climate, so what responsibility do readers bear for the work a text is made to do? The debate plants the flag that McPhee and Deloria will fight over for the rest of the term.

## Facilitation Notes

The room will contain students for whom Genesis is scripture, students for whom it is someone else's scripture, and students encountering it cold; the literary frame protects all three, but say explicitly that analyzing the text's artistry is not a verdict on its sanctity. Genesis 22 should be read aloud and given silence afterward; resist the urge to fill it. Keep the gallery walk's comparative frame respectful of every tradition represented, including the scientific one. The revision workshop portion is brief, so structure it tightly: each pair exchanges drafts and answers two questions only, what is the thesis and where does the argument thin out.

## Connections Forward

Darwin arrives next, and the bridge should be built today: Genesis asks what humans are for, and Darwin offers an account of where we came from that appears to dissolve the question, or relocate it. Ask students to read Darwin while holding the gallery walk's third question: what does this account ask of its hearers?
