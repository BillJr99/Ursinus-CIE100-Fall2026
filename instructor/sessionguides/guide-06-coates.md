# Session Guide 6: Coates, Between the World and Me (Tu 10/6, Th 10/15, Tu 10/20, Th 10/22)

*Instructor-facing session guide. Class meets Tu/Th 1:30 to 2:45 PM (75 minutes). Not for distribution to students.*

## Overview

Coates anchors the longest unit of the semester, four sessions across the fall break, and it asks more of the room than any other text: it is intimate, angry, beautiful, and addressed to a child. The unit's through-lines are the body as the site where abstraction becomes real, the Dream as the machinery of comfortable forgetting, and the letter as a form (connecting back to Adichie and forward to students' own writing). The activities are deliberately quieter than in other units: a private letter exercise, an anonymous campus mapping, and a structured connection from the Dream's machinery to its algorithmic descendants in surveillance technology. Essay 2 runs concurrently through this unit.

## Learning Goals

Students should be able to analyze how Coates makes the body carry philosophical weight, explain the Dream and the work Coates says it does, situate the book in the letter tradition the course has been building, connect the book's account of surveillance and control to contemporary technological systems, and discuss race in a seminar with evidence, care, and honesty.

## Materials and Preparation

Day 1 needs the Essay 2 assignment sheets. Day 2 (after break) needs the anonymous mapping materials: identical sticky notes in two colors and two campus/town outline maps, plus a box for anonymous submission. Day 3 needs nothing special beyond the Mecca passages flagged. Day 4 needs the surveillance-technology case summaries (two or three short, factual descriptions of predictive policing and facial recognition deployments, prepared in advance with sources). Essay 2 first drafts due Day 4.

## Session Arc, Day 1: The Body (Tu 10/6, 75 minutes)

Open with fifteen minutes on the form: a letter to a fifteen year old son, and what that address does that an essay for the public could not, connecting explicitly to Adichie. Spend twenty-five minutes in close reading of passages where the body carries the argument, asking students to find where Coates refuses abstraction and why. Devote fifteen minutes to the Dream: galaxies of suburbs, strawberry shortcake, and the sleep it names. Hand out Essay 2 with ten minutes of brainstorming on its prompts, and close with the private letter exercise setup and five minutes of commonplace writing. Remind students the next meeting is after APEX Day and fall break.

## Session Arc, Day 2: Fear and Safety (Th 10/15, 75 minutes)

Reopen after break with ten minutes of re-grounding in the text. Spend twenty minutes on the schools and streets chapters, where fear organizes childhood. Run the anonymous campus mapping for twenty-five minutes as detailed below. Use fifteen minutes for full group discussion of what the maps show, held carefully, and close with five minutes of exit writing. Essay 2 scaffold deliverables due today; remind students about Writing Fellow conferences.

## Session Arc, Day 3: The Mecca (Tu 10/20, 75 minutes)

Spend twenty-five minutes on Howard as Mecca: what Coates found there, the difference between the university and the Mecca, and the discovery that blackness is a galaxy rather than a single story. Devote twenty-five minutes to a discussion that lands the passage in students' first semester: what they came to college hoping to find, and what a Mecca would mean for them, with the explicit framing that Coates is describing the thing this course is supposed to build, an intellectual community where you are seen. Spend twenty minutes connecting Part II's Prince Jones narrative as the Dream's cost made singular, and close with five minutes of commonplace writing.

## Session Arc, Day 4: The Dream's Machinery (Th 10/22, 75 minutes)

Open with fifteen minutes on Part III and the visit to Mable Jones, attending to the book's refusal of easy hope. Run the algorithmic Dream exercise for thirty minutes as detailed below. Spend twenty minutes on the ending, the struggle as its own meaning, and whether the book's address to one boy succeeds as an address to the country. Reserve ten minutes for Essay 2 first-draft logistics and synthesis of the unit.

## Discussion Question Bank

Why a letter, and why does Coates tell his son he will not comfort him? Where does the body do work that the word racism cannot? Who are the Dreamers, and can someone wake from the Dream by choosing to? What is the difference between Howard the institution and the Mecca? What does Prince Jones's story do to the book's argument that statistics could not? Is the book hopeful, and would hope be a virtue or a betrayal here? What does Coates mean when he says race is the child of racism, not the father?

**Protocol option: Four A's rounds.** Any of this unit's discussion blocks — the Day 1 close reading of the body passages or the Day 3 Mecca discussion are the natural fits — can be run as Four A's rounds instead of open discussion. Groups of four mark the assigned pages silently for the four A's (what the author assumes, what you agree with, what you want to argue with, what you aspire to), then share in rounds, one "A" per round, with each student required to cite the specific passage behind their point; close with an open round on implications and a one-minute debrief on the process. The structure works especially well when this room is polarized or quiet — and this unit produces both — because the rounds distribute airtime evenly and the "agree" and "aspire" rounds guarantee the book is met before it is judged. Students know the protocol from the Four A's reading response. Citation: Gray, J., "Four 'A's Text Protocol," National School Reform Faculty (NSRF), https://www.nsrfharmony.org/wp-content/uploads/2017/10/FourAsTextProtocol-N.pdf.

## Featured Activity Detail

**The Private Letter (assigned Day 1, collected Day 2).** Students write a letter to a real younger person about a fear or a hope tied to their own body, identity, or place in the world. The letter is informal writing, graded for completion only, and students may mark it private to the instructor, an option announced clearly; the exercise's purpose is to learn the form's demands from inside, exactly as the manifesto exercise did for Adichie. Volunteers may share a single sentence on Day 2, never pressured.

**The Anonymous Campus Map (Day 2, 25 minutes).** Two identical campus and town maps go up. On one color of sticky note students mark a place they feel watched, and on the other a place they feel free, submitting anonymously through a box so notes cannot be matched to faces. Post them, then read the maps together: where do the clusters fall, and do all bodies in the room map the same campus? The discussion must be held gently and factually; the maps make visible, without forcing any individual disclosure, the book's claim that bodies experience the same spaces differently. Connect to Coates's geography of fear in Baltimore.

**The Algorithmic Dream (Day 4, 30 minutes).** Coates describes the Dream as machinery: redlining, policing, schools, the whole apparatus that produces comfort in one place and fear in another. Groups receive the prepared case summaries of predictive policing and facial recognition deployments and answer a structured question set: what data trains the system, where did that data come from, what does the system optimize for, and would Coates recognize this as the Dream acquiring new instruments? The unit's point, that historical patterns laundered through data become forecasts, is the course's most important AI-ethics lesson, and it lands here because Coates gives it a body. Require every claim to anchor in either a passage or a case fact.

## Facilitation Notes

This unit asks Black students neither to perform expertise nor to absorb the room's discomfort, and asks white students to engage rather than spectate; the instruments for this are evidence-based norms (claims anchor in passages), the anonymity of the mapping exercise, and your own willingness to sit in hard silences without rescuing the room. Fall break splits the unit, so Day 2 needs real re-entry time. The Prince Jones chapters land hard; signal them in advance without softening them. Watch the participation balance closely across these four sessions, and use the rotating discussion roles deliberately.

## Connections Forward

Genesis follows immediately, and the pivot is naming and inheritance: Coates writes about what is visited upon bodies by an inherited order, and Genesis is the West's oldest story about inheritance, naming, and whose line carries the promise. Essay 2 drafts continue through the Genesis unit, so the texts will cross-pollinate in conferences.
