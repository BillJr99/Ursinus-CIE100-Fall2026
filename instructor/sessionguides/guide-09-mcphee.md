# Session Guide 9: McPhee (Tu 11/10 and Th 11/12)

*Instructor-facing session guide. Class meets Tu/Th 1:30 to 2:45 PM (75 minutes). Not for distribution to students.*

## Overview

McPhee's account of engineering campaigns against rivers and mountains gives the course its case study in hubris with blueprints, and it has a gift no other text on the syllabus has: a local address. The Perkiomen Creek's flooding during Hurricane Ida in 2021 put this region's relationship with water on the front page, and the unit uses that proximity. Day 1 reads McPhee's craft and stakes and runs the local waters mapping exercise; Day 2 stages the Army Corps hearing role-play and hands out Essay 3 with its brainstorming workshop. The unit also begins the course's closing movement, in which Genesis's dominion debate returns wearing a hard hat.

## Learning Goals

Students should be able to analyze McPhee's narrative technique for making engineering dramatic and morally legible, articulate the competing goods at stake in controlling nature (safety, property, ecology, cost, justice across generations), connect the readings to their own region's flood history, and argue a stakeholder position with evidence while recognizing its partiality.

## Materials and Preparation

Day 1 needs printed or projected maps showing local floodplains and the Perkiomen watershed, plus two or three short news excerpts about the 2021 Ida flooding in the Collegeville area, gathered in advance. Day 2 needs the hearing role cards (Corps engineer, longtime resident, ecologist, municipal official, downstream farmer, taxpayer advocate, insurance representative) and the Essay 3 assignment sheets.

## Session Arc, Day 1: The Contest (75 minutes)

Open with ten minutes on McPhee's title and its irony: whether "the control of nature" is a boast, a job title, or a verdict. Spend twenty-five minutes in close reading of passages where the engineers' confidence and the water's indifference collide, attending to McPhee's technique of letting engineers speak in their own proud vocabulary while the prose quietly counts the costs. Run the local waters exercise for twenty-five minutes as detailed below. Close with ten minutes on the question of time, connecting Darwin's deep time to the engineers' fiscal-year time, and five minutes of commonplace writing.

## Session Arc, Day 2: The Hearing (75 minutes)

Open with ten minutes recapping the structural dilemma McPhee documents: every defense raises the stakes of eventual failure, and retreat is politically unspeakable. Run the Army Corps hearing for thirty-five minutes as detailed below. Spend ten minutes debriefing out of role. Hand out Essay 3 and run its brainstorming workshop for fifteen minutes, mapping the three prompts onto the semester's texts on the board, and close with five minutes of exit writing.

## Discussion Question Bank

Is McPhee an admirer or a critic of his engineers, and where does the prose tip its hand? What does it mean that the river is never the antagonist in McPhee's telling, only the fact? Who chose for the people who live behind the levees, and when? What would surrender look like, and why is it unsayable in public meetings? Where has your own community controlled nature, and who holds the receipt? Is managed retreat wisdom or defeat? Does Genesis's "till it and watch it" have anything to say to the Army Corps?

## Featured Activity Detail

**Local Waters (Day 1, 25 minutes).** Project the floodplain map of the Perkiomen watershed beside the 2021 Ida news excerpts. In groups, students answer a structured sequence: where does the water want to go, what have we built in its way, what happened in September 2021, and what has been built or rebuilt since? Students from the region contribute local knowledge; students from elsewhere map the same questions onto their hometowns in a closing round, which reliably surfaces wildfire, drought, and coastal cases that widen the unit beyond rivers. The exercise converts McPhee from travelogue into mirror, and several students discover that they have lived their whole lives inside one of his chapters without knowing it.

**The Army Corps Hearing (Day 2, 35 minutes).** A proposed flood-control project (compose a plausible local one: a levee and channelization project on a Perkiomen tributary after the next Ida) goes to a public hearing. Students draw stakeholder roles, get five minutes of preparation with role cards listing their interests and their best McPhee-grounded arguments, and the hearing runs with formal speaking turns and a student chair. The rule that makes it work: every speaker must use at least one fact or pattern from the reading. The debrief asks which arguments moved people, whose interests had no seat at the table (the river? the next generation?), and what the hearing format itself, with its three minute turns and its yard signs, does to deliberation, a question that quietly opens the McLuhan unit.

## Facilitation Notes

Ida flooding caused real losses in this region, and a student in the room may have lived them; frame the local exercise with that possibility named, and let personal testimony be offered rather than solicited. Keep the hearing from collapsing into caricature by insisting the role cards contain each stakeholder's best arguments, not their stereotypes. Essay 3's hand-out shares Day 2, so protect the workshop's fifteen minutes; the synthesis essay benefits more from early mapping than any other assignment in the course.

## Connections Forward

Deloria asks what the land is, beneath the question of what to do with it, and the bridge is sharp: McPhee's hearing had no chair for the position that the river is sacred. Tell students Deloria will occupy that chair, and ask them to notice their own first reaction to the idea.
