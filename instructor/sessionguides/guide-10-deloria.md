# Session Guide 10: Deloria, God Is Red (Tu 11/17 and Th 11/19)

*Instructor-facing session guide. Class meets Tu/Th 1:30 to 2:45 PM (75 minutes). Not for distribution to students.*

## Overview

Deloria reframes everything the course has read about nature by relocating the sacred from time to space, from history's arc to the land underfoot, and he does it as critique: of Christianity's temporal orientation, of America's treatment of Native peoples and places, and of the assumption that religion is primarily a set of beliefs rather than a relationship to place. The unit takes the argument seriously enough to test it on the students' own geography, through the sacred places mapping, and on the college's own ground, through the land acknowledgment analysis. The withdrawal deadline falls on Day 1 and Essay 3 scaffolds are due Day 2.

## Learning Goals

Students should be able to explain Deloria's distinction between temporal and spatial orientations in religion and trace its consequences, situate the argument in its historical context of dispossession and repatriation struggles, examine their own relationships to place using Deloria's categories, and evaluate land acknowledgment practices with the text's own standards rather than reflexive cynicism or reflexive piety.

## Materials and Preparation

Day 1 needs index cards for the sacred places exercise. Day 2 needs the prepared materials on local Indigenous history: a short factual summary of Lenape presence in this region and the relevant treaty and dispossession history, assembled in advance from reliable sources, plus the text of two or three real land acknowledgments from peer institutions (and Ursinus's own, if one exists, which should be verified before class). A brief factual note on NAGPRA and one repatriation case rounds out the kit.

## Session Arc, Day 1: Space Against Time (75 minutes)

Open with ten minutes on who Deloria was and the book's moment, enough context that the argument reads as a position in a struggle rather than an abstraction. Spend twenty-five minutes in close reading of the temporal-versus-spatial distinction: what each orientation makes of land, of history, of newcomers, with students charting the contrast on the board from quotations. Run the sacred places exercise for twenty-five minutes as detailed below. Close with ten minutes asking the day's hard question, whether the class's own educations have been entirely temporal, and five minutes of commonplace writing. Note the withdrawal deadline aloud.

## Session Arc, Day 2: This Ground (75 minutes)

Open with fifteen minutes on the local history materials: whose land this region was, by what sequence it changed hands, and where Lenape communities are now, delivered factually. Run the land acknowledgment analysis for thirty minutes as detailed below. Spend fifteen minutes on the NAGPRA case as the question of what taking the sacred seriously costs institutions in practice. Reserve ten minutes for Essay 3 scaffold collection and conferencing logistics, and five for exit writing.

## Discussion Question Bank

What does Deloria mean by calling Christianity a temporal religion, and is the characterization fair? What can a spatial religion not export, and what does that imply about conquest and conversion? Can a place be sacred to someone with no tradition, or is that longing for something his argument says you cannot improvise? What would it mean for an institution, rather than a person, to regard land as sacred? When is a land acknowledgment an act and when is it a screensaver? What does Deloria add to the dominion-versus-stewardship debate that neither side had?

## Featured Activity Detail

**Sacred Places (Day 1, 25 minutes).** Each student writes on a card a place that is, in whatever sense they can defend, sacred to them, with three sentences on why. In small groups, students share and then classify their own cases with Deloria's categories: is the place sacred because of what happened there (temporal: the site of a memory) or because of what it is (spatial: the place itself, prior to any story)? The reliable discovery is that most students' sacred places are temporal, sanctified by autobiography, and the group can then ask what it would even feel like for a place to be sacred in Deloria's stronger sense, and whether anything in their lives approaches it. The exercise gives students a stake in a distinction that otherwise stays bloodless.

**The Land Acknowledgment Analysis (Day 2, 30 minutes).** Groups receive the local history summary and the collected land acknowledgment texts. The task has three stages: first, extract from Deloria the standards an acknowledgment would have to meet to be more than ceremony (naming the people and the taking, present-tense relationship, consequences in institutional behavior); second, audit the sample acknowledgments against those standards line by line; third, draft either a revised acknowledgment for the college or a one paragraph argument that the form is unrescuable and something else is owed instead. Groups present and defend. The exercise threads the needle the unit requires: it takes the critique of performativity seriously without letting cynicism become its own comfortable exit, and it ends with students having made an actual proposal about the actual ground under the classroom.

## Facilitation Notes

There may be Native students in the room, and the same norm from the Coates unit applies doubly: no one is asked to speak for a people. The local history segment must be prepared from reliable sources and delivered as fact rather than sentiment; if Ursinus's institutional acknowledgment status is uncertain, verify it beforehand rather than guessing in class. Guard against the two easy failure modes, piety that performs agreement without thought and cynicism that performs sophistication without stakes; the drafting exercise is the structural defense against both. Essay 3 scaffolds are due Day 2.

## Connections Forward

McLuhan closes the question of where we live by changing its terms: after land comes the media environment, the place made of signals where students arguably spend more waking hours than on any ground. The bridge question to pose at the end of Day 2: if Deloria is right that where you are shapes what you worship, what is shaped by living nowhere in particular, online?
