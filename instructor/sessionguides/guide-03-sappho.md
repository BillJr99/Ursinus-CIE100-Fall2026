# Session Guide 3: Sappho (Th 9/10 and Tu 9/15)

*Instructor-facing session guide. Class meets Tu/Th 1:30 to 2:45 PM (75 minutes). Not for distribution to students.*

## Overview

Sappho arrives as the semester's lesson in how texts survive, and how interpretation works when most of the evidence is missing. The first session makes fragmentation physical through the papyrus reconstruction workshop; the second connects the fate of Sappho's corpus to the way art survives algorithmic circulation today, and turns to what the poems actually say about desire, memory, and the personal voice. Students should leave understanding that every translation and every reconstruction is an argument, a lesson that pays off in every subsequent unit.

## Learning Goals

Students should be able to describe how Sappho's texts reached us and why that history shapes interpretation, perform a close reading of a lyric fragment with attention to what is present and absent, articulate how translation embodies interpretive choices, and connect the fragmentation of ancient texts to the fragmentation of contemporary media artifacts.

## Materials and Preparation

For Day 1: print copies of a short original poem template, scissors, envelopes (each group receives another group's torn fragments). Prepare two or three translations of the same Sappho fragment (Barnard alongside one other translator) for the comparison stations. For Day 2: students bring their viral-fragment examples from the writing prompt. The Essay 1 first draft is due on Day 2; reserve the final minutes for logistics about Writing Fellow conferences with Vienna.

## Session Arc, Day 1: Fragments (75 minutes)

Open with ten minutes on the transmission history: nine books in the Library of Alexandria reduced to one or two complete poems and a sea of fragments, quoted by grammarians and recovered from mummy cartonnage. Run the papyrus workshop for thirty minutes as detailed below. Spend twenty minutes on translation comparison stations, where pairs read the same fragment in two translations and list every difference as an interpretive decision. Close with ten minutes of full-group discussion of fragment 31 or another rich fragment, and five minutes of commonplace book writing on a phrase that survives beautifully without its context.

## Session Arc, Day 2: Survival (75 minutes)

Open with fifteen minutes of close reading of two or three fragments chosen by students from their prompts, attending to the lyric "I" and the named beloveds. Run the viral fragment comparison for twenty-five minutes as detailed below. Spend twenty minutes on the larger question of who controls survival, from Byzantine copyists to recommendation systems, and what responsibilities readers have to fragments. Reserve ten minutes for Essay 1 draft logistics and Writing Fellow scheduling, and five for exit writing.

## Discussion Question Bank

What does it do to your reading to know that the gaps on the page are wounds rather than choices? Whose decisions, across twenty-six centuries, determined which lines you are holding? Is Barnard's clean modern English an act of recovery or of invention? Why has the lyric first person, the voice that says I want and I remember, proven so durable? What do we owe a poet whose privacy we are reading? Is a fragment torn from context still the poet's work, and is a fifteen second clip still the artist's song?

## Featured Activity Detail

**The Papyrus Workshop (Day 1, 30 minutes).** Each student writes a short poem of eight to ten lines on a personal memory, then physically tears it into six to eight pieces and removes two pieces, which go in their pocket. The remaining fragments go into an envelope and pass to another group, which must reconstruct a reading of the poem: not guess the missing words, but argue for an interpretation from what survives, exactly as a philologist must. Groups present their reconstructions, and then the authors reveal the withheld fragments. The reliable, productive shock is how confident the reconstructions were and how the missing pieces change everything; name that experience as the epistemic condition of all Sappho scholarship, and indeed of interpreting any incomplete evidence, a thread that runs to Darwin's fossil record later in the term.

**The Viral Fragment Comparison (Day 2, 25 minutes).** In groups, students present the viral clips they brought and analyze them with the same questions applied to Sappho: what context was stripped, what made this fragment survive when the whole did not, who profited from its circulation, and does the fragment misrepresent the original? The conversation about artists whose careers are reshaped by fifteen seconds of a three minute song lands with genuine force, and gives students a non-anachronistic way to care about textual transmission.

## Facilitation Notes

Sappho's poems address women with desire, and students may ask about her sexuality; the honest scholarly answer is that the poems are evidence of expressed desire and the rest is inference, which is itself a useful lesson in arguing from fragments. Keep the papyrus workshop's poems low-stakes by telling students in advance that the poem will be torn and read by others, so no one writes anything too raw. The Essay 1 draft is due Day 2; expect some distraction and front-load the intellectually demanding work.

## Connections Forward

The Islamic mystic poets continue the lyric of longing with the beloved transfigured into the divine; ask students to carry fragment 31's physiology of desire into that reading. The question of who controls survival and circulation returns with McLuhan.
