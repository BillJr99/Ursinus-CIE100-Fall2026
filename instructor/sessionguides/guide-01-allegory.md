# Session Guide 1: Plato, Allegory of the Cave (Tu 8/25)

*Instructor-facing session guide. Class meets Tu/Th 1:30 to 2:45 PM (75 minutes). Not for distribution to students.*

## Overview

The semester opens with the most durable image in Western philosophy, and our job is to make first-year students feel that they already live inside it. The session moves from careful reconstruction of the allegory's machinery (chains, fire, puppeteers, shadows, ascent, return) to the Algorithmic Cave activity, in which students audit their own recommendation feeds as a candidate modern cave. The goal is not to flatter the comparison but to test it: the class should leave arguing about where the analogy holds and where it breaks, which is exactly the disposition CIE wants to build on day one of seminar discussion.

## Learning Goals

By the end of this session, students should be able to reconstruct the allegory's elements and their referents in Plato's epistemology, evaluate a contemporary analogy against the text rather than against vibes, and practice the seminar norms (evidence on the table, building on peers, disagreeing with ideas rather than people) that will govern the semester.

## Materials and Preparation

Bring a whiteboard diagram or printed image of the cave (the Saenredam engraving from the syllabus banner works well). Students were asked in the writing prompt to bring one concrete example from their own feed. Prepare an anonymous index-card option for students reluctant to share their algorithmic profile aloud. Have the four CIE core questions posted visibly; they will anchor every unit.

## Session Arc (75 minutes)

The session opens with a five minute welcome and a recap of seminar norms established at the Friday all-sections meeting. Spend the next ten minutes on a silent diagram exercise: each student labels the parts of the cave on a handout and writes one sentence per element about what it represents, which surfaces the range of readings before discussion begins. The following twenty minutes are close reading in pairs and then full group: the moment of liberation, the pain of the light, and especially the return, asking why the freed prisoner goes back down and what Plato predicts will happen. The Algorithmic Cave activity occupies the next twenty-five minutes as detailed below. Reserve ten minutes for a synthesis discussion mapping the activity back onto the text, and close with five minutes of exit writing for the commonplace book: one quotation from Plato and one sentence on where the student suspects they are still chained.

## Discussion Question Bank

Why are the prisoners described as "like us"? What does it mean that they would award prizes to those best at predicting the shadows, and what is the modern equivalent of those prizes? Is the ascent something done to the prisoner or by the prisoner, and does that matter? Why does the returning prisoner look ridiculous, and why might the prisoners kill him? Is the philosopher obligated to return, and who, if anyone, plays the role of the one who frees the first prisoner? What would count as the sun in our own setting, or is the allegory's confidence in a single reality the part that has aged worst?

## Featured Activity Detail

**The Algorithmic Cave (25 minutes).** Working in groups of four, students pool the feed examples they brought and construct an explicit mapping: who or what plays the prisoners, the chains, the fire, the puppeteers, the shadows, and the ascent in the world of recommendation algorithms. Each group must also identify one place where the mapping fails, since the failure points (prisoners who helped build the cave, an absence of any puppeteer with intent, the possibility that there is no outside) are where the best discussion lives. Groups report out by writing their mapping and their disanalogy on the board. Press the class on the prizes passage: likes, streaks, and engagement metrics map uncannily well onto honors for shadow-prediction, and students who realize this aloud tend to remember it for the rest of the semester. End by asking who profits from the wall, which sets up Adichie's interest in invisible conditioning two days later.

## Facilitation Notes

Resist letting the session become a technology gripe circle; the text must stay on the table, and every claim about algorithms should be forced back through a passage. Some students will defend their feeds, and that defense is pedagogically precious: Plato's prisoners also defend the shadows, and naming that parallel gently (not triumphantly) models charitable disagreement. First-years may be nervous on day one of real seminar discussion, so the pair-share before full group matters. Keep notes on who speaks for your participation records, and privately encourage one or two quiet students whose written prompts were strong.

## Connections Forward

Adichie supplies the next case of invisible conditioning, this time gendered rather than perceptual; flag the continuity explicitly. The cave returns at the end of the semester when Forster's Vashti lives in a literal underground cell lit by artificial light, and it is worth telling students now that we will close the course back in a cave, because the promise of that return gives the semester an architecture they can feel.
