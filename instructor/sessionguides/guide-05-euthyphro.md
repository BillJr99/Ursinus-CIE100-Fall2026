# Session Guide 5: Plato, Euthyphro (Tu 9/29 and Th 10/1)

*Instructor-facing session guide. Class meets Tu/Th 1:30 to 2:45 PM (75 minutes). Not for distribution to students.*

## Overview

Euthyphro gives the class its first sustained encounter with the Socratic method as a practice rather than a reputation, and its central dilemma remains one of the sharpest tools ever forged for examining authority. The first session works through the dialogue and its definitional failures, then puts the method in students' hands with a fishbowl exercise on terms they use daily. The second session brings the dilemma forward: to law, to platform rules, and, in this course's signature move, to AI systems, asking whether an answer is right because the model says so or whether the model says so because it is right. Euthyphro's family situation also opens the loyalty-versus-justice question that Essay 2 will formalize.

## Learning Goals

Students should be able to reconstruct the dilemma and explain why each horn undermines Euthyphro's confidence, practice Socratic questioning on contemporary value terms, apply the dilemma to modern authorities including algorithmic ones, and articulate why a dialogue that ends in failure might still count as progress.

## Materials and Preparation

Prepare the fishbowl term cards for Day 1: fair, authentic, cringe, cancel-worthy, toxic, and similar terms students use with unexamined confidence. For Day 2, prepare the mock-hearing roles for the modern dilemma exercise, and have one or two saved transcripts of an AI assistant answering an ethical question, to put on the projector as evidence rather than relying on a live demo. Essay 1 final drafts are due Day 1; collect revision memos.

## Session Arc, Day 1: What Is Piety? (75 minutes)

Open with ten minutes on the frame story, which students often skim: Euthyphro is prosecuting his own father, and Socrates is on his way to face the charges that will kill him, so the conversation about piety is happening between a man too certain and a man about to die for asking questions. Spend twenty-five minutes tracing the definitional attempts and their collapses, having students diagram on the board why each definition fails, until the dilemma stands alone. Run the Socratic fishbowl for twenty-five minutes as detailed below. Close with ten minutes on aporia, asking whether the dialogue fails or succeeds, and five minutes of commonplace writing.

## Session Arc, Day 2: The Dilemma's Descendants (75 minutes)

Open with ten minutes restating the dilemma precisely, because precision here pays for the whole session. Spend twenty minutes on the loyalty question through the frame story: was Euthyphro right to prosecute his father, connecting to modern whistleblowing cases students can name. Run the algorithmic Euthyphro exercise for thirty minutes as detailed below. Close with ten minutes of synthesis on what the unit teaches about trusting any authority, and five minutes of exit writing that seeds Essay 2 option three.

## Discussion Question Bank

Why does Plato have this conversation happen on the courthouse steps? Is Euthyphro's confidence the real target of the dialogue? State the dilemma in your own words: what exactly is wrong with each horn? Can the dilemma be escaped, and what would it cost? Was Euthyphro right to prosecute his father, and what do we owe family when family does wrong? If you trust an authority you cannot interrogate, which horn are you living on? Does the dialogue's failure to define piety mean the conversation was worthless?

## Featured Activity Detail

**The Socratic Fishbowl (Day 1, 25 minutes).** Two volunteers sit in the center; one asserts a definition of a drawn term (fair, authentic, cancel-worthy), and the other plays Socrates, asking only questions, seeking counterexamples, and never asserting. The outer circle tracks the argument and may tag in. Rotate two or three terms. The reliable discovery is that students cannot define the words they wield most confidently, which is precisely Euthyphro's position, and experiencing the method from both chairs teaches more about the dialogue than any summary. Debrief on how it felt to be questioned, and on the difference between Socratic questioning and merely being difficult.

**The Algorithmic Euthyphro (Day 2, 30 minutes).** Pose the updated dilemma: is an answer right because the AI system says so, or does the system say so because it is right? Stage it as a hearing. One group argues the first horn (the system's outputs constitute a kind of authority for millions of users who never check them), one argues the second (the system merely aggregates human judgments, so the standard lies elsewhere, in the training data, which reopens the question of whose judgments those were), and a third group serves as Socratic examiners armed with the projected transcripts. The exercise lands the course's AI-literacy goal precisely where a 2,400 year old text put it: authority that cannot give an account of itself is the thing Socrates refused to accept. Close by asking students where, in their own lives, they have outsourced the question "is this right?" to something that cannot answer "why?".

## Facilitation Notes

The fishbowl needs a firm hand on tone: Socrates is relentless but not cruel, and the examiner role should be framed as a service to the definition, not an attack on the definer. Religious students sometimes hear the dilemma as an attack on faith; the historical note that medieval theologians of several traditions engaged it seriously and devised sophisticated responses keeps the room open. Day 1 doubles as the Essay 1 final due date, so expect a tired room and put the fishbowl, which generates its own energy, in the middle rather than the end.

## Connections Forward

Coates begins Tu 10/6 and Essay 2 is handed out that day; the bridge is the frame story. Euthyphro confronts inherited authority in the name of justice, and Coates writes to his son about a country's inherited Dream. Tell students to carry the dilemma into Coates: is the Dream good because America loves it, or does America love it because it is good, and who pays while the question goes unasked?
